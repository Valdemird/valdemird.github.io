The patterns sheet of my lab has a hopper in a pen, two drones hanging over a one-way platform and a walker at the end. Press T and every enemy gets a label over its head with the state it's in. While it warns, the label shows the attack's name and a bar filling up. When it's open, the label turns green. The sheet's first note: green means it's your turn.

In my recorded sessions, 12 of the 18 hits I took on that sheet came from the drones. Six were shots and six were body contact. The drone has 2 hit points, the fewest of anything in the lab that attacks. On the next sheet, the guard's, 8 of my swings bounced off a shield.

<Callout type="important" title="The thesis">
Enemy variety doesn't come from health or damage. It comes from the question each enemy asks and the answer it demands: jump, dodge, wait, go around. Build every enemy on the same skeleton and spend your design on two things only: what it asks, and where it leaves the door open.

<h2 id="what-is-an-enemy-pattern">What is an enemy attack pattern?</h2>

An enemy attack pattern is the fixed sequence an enemy runs through every time it attacks: notice the player, warn, attack, recover. Because it's fixed, the player can learn it. Because it ends in a recovery, the player has somewhere to hit back. Every enemy in my lab, including the boss, runs the same skeleton:

<MermaidDiagram title="The skeleton every enemy in the lab shares" chart={`
graph LR
  A[patrol] -->|sees you| B["WARNING<br/>fixed length"]
  B --> C[ATTACK]
  C --> D["RECOVERY<br/>your turn"]
  D --> A
`} />

Mike Stout describes enemies in action games by the questions they ask in the moment, and boils them down to two: "Can you avoid my damage?" and "Can you hit me?"<Sidenote>From his 2015 [Game Developer piece on enemy attacks and telegraphing](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/enemy-attacks-and-telegraphing).</Sidenote> The warning asks the first one, [as the telegraph post covered](/blog/telegraphing-attacks/). The recovery asks the second. What makes one enemy different from another is the specific form of the first question, and the shape of the window that answers the second.

<h2 id="patterns-godot">How to build enemy patterns in Godot 4</h2>

The base class `Enemy` owns everything shared: health, the three boxes ([hitbox, hurtbox and body](/blog/hitbox-vs-hurtbox/)), the hit reaction, the warning and the state variable. A new enemy writes one function, `_think()`, with a `match` on its state. Two of them, side by side, show how little each one needs to be different. The comments are in Spanish because the whole project is.

<Tabs labels={["Hopper: a fixed-time leap", "Drone: aim that locks halfway"]}>
<TabPanel>

The hopper's leap is real ballistics with a fixed vertical speed and a fixed gravity, so the flight always takes the same time: 2 × 620 / 2400, about 0.52 s. Only the horizontal speed changes, solved from where you were standing at takeoff. Move after it leaves the ground and it lands on empty floor.

<CodeBlock filename="addons/combat_kit/enemies/hopper.gd">
```gdscript
func _leap() -> void:
	# Tiro parabólico: con la velocidad vertical y la gravedad fijas, el tiempo de
	# vuelo es constante; la velocidad horizontal se despeja de la distancia.
	var flight := 2.0 * jump_speed / GRAVITY
	var landing := global_position.x + clampf(to_target().x, -max_hop, max_hop)
	if leash > 0.0:
		landing = clampf(landing, _spawn_position.x - leash, _spawn_position.x + leash)
	velocity = Vector2((landing - global_position.x) / flight, -jump_speed)
	contact.attack = &"salto"
	release(&"leap")
```
</CodeBlock>

</TabPanel>
<TabPanel>

The drone tracks you for the first half of its 0.65 s warning, then stops. From that moment the line it draws is a promise: the shot goes there. A shot that corrects until the last frame can't be dodged by skill, and the script's header says so.

Six of the drones' hits on me were shots. Each one was aimed at where I stood when its aim locked, and I was still in the way when it arrived. Standing still to line up a hit on the hopper is exactly the wrong answer while a drone is mid-warning above you.

The green labels come from the debug view, and the list of what counts as "your turn" is one line:

<CodeBlock filename="addons/combat_kit/debug/combat_debug.gd">
```gdscript
		if enemy.state == &"windup":
			color = BP.ACCENT
			label = "%s · AVISO %s" % [enemy.display_name, String(enemy.attack_name).to_upper()]
		elif enemy.state in [&"recover", &"dazed", &"staggered", &"land"]:
			color = BP.OK   # ventana de castigo
```
</CodeBlock>

Four state names, across five enemies and a boss. `recover` after an attack, `dazed` when the charger hits a wall, `land` after the hopper's leap, `staggered` when the boss's poise breaks. Designing an enemy is mostly deciding which of those it gets and for how long.

<h2 id="openings-guard">Openings: the guard that can't be mashed</h2>

The guard walks behind a shield. Hit it from the front and you get a metal clang, no damage and a recoil 1.9 times stronger than usual. The sheet's note says what to do instead: don't insist, provoke its thrust, dodge it, punish.

The shield isn't a second collision box. It's a function on the guard's hurtbox that looks at where the hit came from and what state the guard is in:

<CodeBlock filename="addons/combat_kit/enemies/guard.gd">
```gdscript
## ¿Para el escudo este golpe? Sólo los tajos que vienen de frente y con la guardia alta.
func _blocks(hit: Hit) -> bool:
	if hit.kind != &"slash" or state == &"recover":
		return false
	if hit.direction.y > 0.5:
		return false   # desde arriba: el escudo no cubre la cabeza
	if not is_instance_valid(hit.source) or not hit.source is Node2D:
		return false
	var attacker_side := signf((hit.source as Node2D).global_position.x - global_position.x)
	return attacker_side == float(facing)
```
</CodeBlock>

Read the `return false` lines as a list of openings, and add the one that lives in its movement code:

1. During `recover`, the 0.85 s after its thrust, the guard is down.
2. A downward strike (`hit.direction.y > 0.5`) gets past it: the shield doesn't cover the head, so [a pogo](/blog/pogo-attack/) works.
3. From behind, `attacker_side` doesn't match `facing`.
4. Turning around costs it 0.45 s, so dashing through it and hitting its back gives you a free window.

A guard like this changes what the attack button means. Against a walker you press attack. Against a guard you decide *when* to press it. The lab's sheet text calls that concept the basis of any boss, and [the boss post](/blog/boss-design/) is where it gets tested.

<h2 id="combat-chess">Two enemies together: DOOM's combat chess</h2>

The last note on the guard sheet, "pick which one goes first", is a small version of what id Software called combat chess in DOOM (2016). Tommy Thompson's [write-up of that game's AI](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/cyber-demons-the-ai-of-doom-2016-) describes how mixing demons "creates what id refers to as combat chess: where your job is to figure out which demon to smash first". Each archetype has a role, for example "imps maintain their distance to enable them to throw fireballs", which is the drone's whole job in my lab.

The useful part for a small game is the arithmetic. Five enemies alone are five situations. A walker under a drone is a sixth, and nobody wrote code for it: the walker asks you to fight on the ground, the drone asks you not to stand still while you do. That's how the lab's sheet text puts it: one enemy is interesting alone, two types together create a new situation without programming anything.

<h2 id="when-to-use">When a new enemy type helps, and when it doesn't</h2>

| Idea | Worth it? |
|---|---|
| An enemy that asks a new question (jump, dodge, wait, go around) | Yes. That's what each of the five is for. |
| The same enemy with more health or damage | No. It only makes the same fight longer. |
| Two enemies you already have, together | Often better than a new one. A walker under a drone is a different fight from either. |
| An enemy with no opening | No. Even the guard has four, and the boss has a recovery after every attack. |
| An enemy that follows you across the level | No. The hopper and the drone stay within 200 px of where they spawned. |
| An aimed attack that tracks until it fires | No. The drone stops aiming halfway through its warning. |

<Collapsible title="Leashes: why the hopper and the drone don't chase you">
Both have a `leash` of 200 px around their spawn point. The hopper clamps where it aims its leap, the drone clamps where it tries to hover. The hopper's header explains the bug it prevents: without the leash, a player standing on the wall of its pen could drag it across the whole level, and the encounter would stop happening where it was designed. The drone's says a flyer that follows you across the level is a nuisance, not a challenge. Each fight on a sheet stays where it was placed, which is the only reason two enemies can be combined on purpose.
</Collapsible>

<h2 id="quick-answers">Quick answers</h2>

<h3 id="good-enemy-pattern">What makes a good enemy attack pattern?</h3>

A clear question, a warning of fixed length, and a recovery window after the attack. In my lab every enemy follows patrol, warning, attack, recovery; what changes is what the attack asks of you and how long the opening lasts.

<h3 id="what-is-an-opening">What is an opening in combat design?</h3>

A window where the enemy can't defend or answer and the player can hit it freely. My lab's debug view marks four states as openings: recovery after an attack, dazed, landing, and staggered. The drone's opening is plainer, the 1.5 s between shots.

<h3 id="how-many-enemy-types">How many enemy types does a game need?</h3>

Fewer than it seems, if each asks a different question. My lab uses five plus a boss, and the combinations (a walker under a drone, a guard with a drone overhead) add situations without new code.

<h3 id="shielded-enemy">How do you make a shielded enemy fair?</h3>

Give it openings that each teach a different skill. The lab's guard opens after its thrust, from behind, from above, and to a dash through it.

Go back to the patterns sheet with T on and watch the hopper: after every leap its label goes green for 0.7 s, whether you use it or not. The drones never turn green. Their opening is the 1.5 s between shots, and you only get it if you're still moving when the shot arrives. Twelve hits says I mostly wasn't.
