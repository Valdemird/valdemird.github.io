On November 15 I started a platformer in Godot. Before I drew a single level I built a lab: one long blueprint where every sheet isolates a single technique, and the T key switches it off while you play. It's the idea behind [my game feel post](/blog/game-feel-on-the-web/), pointed one layer down. That post was about juice, what the game shows you after you act. This series is about what decides whether your action works at all.

The lab records every session: every jump, every press that didn't turn into a jump, every time one of these assists stepped in. While putting this post together I had Claude total up four days of my own sessions. 130 of them, 4,595 jumps. 363 of those jumps happened only because of two timers I had stopped thinking about the day I wrote them.

<Callout type="important" title="The thesis">
A good assist is one the player never notices. Coyote time and the jump buffer are two timers, 100 and 120 milliseconds, and their whole job is to side with what the player meant over what the hitbox says happened.
</Callout>

<h2 id="coyote-time">Coyote time: jumping after the ground is gone</h2>

Coyote time is a short window after you walk off a ledge during which the game still lets you jump, as if you were standing on it. In my lab the window is 100 ms. The name comes from Wile E. Coyote, who runs off the cliff and hangs in the air until he looks down. Celeste ships the same number: it's in the published player code as `JumpGraceTime = 0.1f`<Sidenote>Line 48 of [Player.cs](https://github.com/NoelFB/Celeste/blob/master/Source/Player/Player.cs), the player code the Celeste team put on GitHub.</Sidenote>.

It exists because players judge the edge with their eyes, and they jump as late as they can on purpose: the later you leave, the farther you get. For a frame or two after the collision box has left the ledge, the character still looks like it's standing on it. Without coyote time, that press arrives one frame late, the character drops, and the player blames the game. They're right to.

This is sheet 05 of the lab. In the clip the hero runs and jumps a beat *after* leaving each ledge: watch for the green `COYOTE +ms` tag, then for the moment T switches it off. Or open the second tab and try it yourself.

<h3 id="coyote-time-godot">Coyote time in Godot 4: the code</h3>

The code is three lines from three places in the player. The comments are in Spanish because the whole project is.

<CodeBlock filename="addons/platformer_kit/player/player.gd">
```gdscript
# Tras moverse, cada tick. Mientras se pisa suelo, el coyote está "cargado".
if on_floor:
	_coyote = params.coyote_time if Lab.on(&"coyote_time") else 0.0

# En _tick_timers(), cada tick:
_coyote = maxf(_coyote - delta, 0.0)

# En _try_jump(). COYOTE TIME: se puede saltar si se está en el suelo... o si se
# dejó hace menos de `coyote_time` segundos. El jugador ve la cornisa bajo sus pies
# unos frames después de haberla abandonado; el juego le da la razón.
if on_floor or _coyote > 0.0:
	_do_jump(&"ground" if on_floor else &"coyote")
```
</CodeBlock>

The line that matters isn't in that block. When a jump starts, `_begin_rise()` sets `_coyote` back to zero. Forget it and the timer is still running while you rise, so a second press in the first 100 ms of a jump becomes a free double jump. Coyote time refills only while you're standing on something, and it's spent the moment you leave by jumping.

| Situation | Coyote time? |
|---|---|
| Running jumps off ledges, the bread and butter of the genre | Yes. It's the case it was invented for. |
| Walking off a ledge on purpose to drop down | Harmless. It only matters if you press jump. |
| A second press right after a real jump | Never. Zero the timer when the jump starts. |
| Precision games that ask for exact edges | Keep it short. 100 ms is what Celeste ships. |

<h2 id="jump-buffer">Jump buffer: pressing too early counts too</h2>

A jump buffer remembers a jump press made shortly before landing and fires the jump the instant you touch down. In my lab it holds the press for 120 ms. It's coyote time's mirror: coyote time forgives a press that comes late, and the buffer forgives one that comes early.

Without it, the press you sent is simply gone. You pressed, nothing happened, and from your side of the screen the button didn't work. It shows most when you chain jumps: across a row of pillars you start pressing on the way down, because that's the rhythm the level asks for.

Sheet 06 is pillars over spikes. In the clip every press comes 70 ms before a landing. With the buffer the hero bounces all the way across. After T, the same presses come back as `× PRONTO` (too early): it lands on the pillar without jumping, keeps running, and drops into the spikes.

<h3 id="jump-buffer-godot">Jump buffer in Godot 4: the code</h3>

<CodeBlock filename="addons/platformer_kit/player/player.gd">
```gdscript
# JUMP BUFFER: no se actúa sobre la pulsación; se RECUERDA durante unos ms.
if Input.is_action_just_pressed(&"jump"):
	_jump_buffer = params.jump_buffer_time if Lab.on(&"jump_buffer") else delta * 0.5

# En _tick_timers(), cada tick:
_jump_buffer = maxf(_jump_buffer - delta, 0.0)

# En _try_jump(): sin una pulsación reciente no hay nada que decidir.
if _jump_buffer <= 0.0:
	return
```
</CodeBlock>

My favorite part is the `else`. With the buffer off, the press is still stored, just for half a tick (`delta * 0.5`), which means "only this exact frame counts". That's what a naive `if Input.is_action_just_pressed(&"jump"): jump()` does anyway. So the T key compares the technique against the code you'd write by default, not against a version someone broke on purpose to make the technique look good.

Where the buffer earns its place:

- Chained jumps: pillars, stomping enemies, anything with a rhythm.
- Any action the player can request while the game can't do it yet: a dash during a recovery, an attack while another one is still swinging. The combat half of my lab buffers attacks the same way, down to the `delta * 0.5`.
- Keep it short. A buffer that's too long jumps after the player has already decided not to, and then the game is the one that feels late.

<h2 id="the-pattern">The pattern underneath: don't react, remember</h2>

Both techniques have the same shape. You don't act on the input when it arrives. You remember when it arrived, and every tick you ask one question: is there a recent press, and can I jump right now? Coyote time stretches the "can I jump" half. The buffer stretches the "recent press" half.

```text
              the ledge ends                       the ground arrives
                    │                                      │
 ── floor ──────────┼────────────── air ───────────────────┼── floor ──
                    ├── coyote: 100 ms ──┤     ├── buffer: 120 ms ──┤
                    press here: still a jump   press here: jumps on landing
```

There's a funny line in my own numbers. The lab exists to switch these things off and feel the difference, and across 130 sessions I died with a technique switched off exactly twice. I built a machine for feeling the gap and then did what every player does: left the help on and forgot it was there.

<Collapsible title="How the lab knows you would have fallen">
With the technique off, the player still measures how long ago it left the floor. If you press jump inside the window the technique would have given you, it doesn't jump, but it reports the miss so the lab can draw `× TARDE 49 ms` over your head:

<CodeBlock filename="addons/platformer_kit/player/player.gd">
```gdscript
elif _since_jump_press == 0.0 and _walked_off and not Lab.on(&"coyote_time") \
		and _since_left_floor <= params.coyote_time:
	# Didáctico: con la técnica apagada, avisamos de que el coyote lo habría salvado.
	assist_note.emit(&"coyote_miss", int(_since_left_floor * 1000.0))
```
</CodeBlock>

The same signal, with technique on, becomes `coyote_hit`. That's what the session recorder counts, and where the 152 at the top of this post comes from: jumps that only happened because the window was open.
</Collapsible>

<h2 id="quick-answers">Quick answers</h2>

<h3 id="how-long-coyote-time">How long should coyote time be?</h3>

100 ms works: it's what my lab uses and what Celeste ships. That's six frames at 60 fps, enough to cover a late reaction and short enough that nobody sees the character jump off thin air.

<h3 id="how-long-jump-buffer">How long should a jump buffer be?</h3>

My lab uses 120 ms. Keep it in that range: a longer buffer starts jumping after the player has already changed their mind.

<h3 id="coyote-double-jump">Can coyote time give a free double jump?</h3>

Only if you forget to reset it. Zero the coyote timer when a jump starts and refill it only while the character stands on the floor.

<h3 id="coyote-and-buffer-together">Do coyote time and the jump buffer work together?</h3>

Yes, and they don't interfere. They stretch opposite ends of the same check: the buffer keeps the press alive, and coyote time keeps the "can jump" alive.

Go back to the coyote sheet, switch the technique off and try to clear the 6u gap. You'll start leaving the ledge earlier without deciding to, and the run feels worse even when you make it. That's what the 100 ms buys. The jumps it saves are the smaller part. The bigger part is that you get to keep jumping as late as your eyes tell you to.
