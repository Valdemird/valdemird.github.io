In June I shipped a [16-module wiki on agentic systems](/learn/agentic-systems/) on this site. I didn't write it by hand. Claude Code workflows drafted and cross-checked it, and then I studied the result until I understood it well enough to argue with it.

Six days after the first commit, a second pass came back. It was an adversarial fact-check where independent agents had to confirm each claim against the web, and it found 20 confirmed claim errors and 26 source issues across the modules. One module credited RLAIF to Google in 2023. The term comes from Anthropic's Constitutional AI paper, from 2022.

Every one of those errors had already passed the first cross-check. On the page they read as smoothly as the correct claims around them. The only way to find them was to go back to the sources.

This post had one of those errors too. I'll get to it.

**You learn faster when you build.** Building forces a different relationship with knowledge: you make the decisions instead of watching someone else make them.

## The tutorial trap

Tutorials feel productive. You follow along, everything works, you understand each step. But there's a hidden cost: you're learning the *instructor's* decisions, not making your own.

The critical difference: when you build, you encounter the *messy middle*, the part between "I understand the concept" and "it works in production." That messy middle is where real learning happens.

<Callout type="important" title="The builder's edge">
Tutorials teach you what works. Building teaches you what breaks, and that's the knowledge that actually matters in production.
</Callout>

## What the research actually measured

A [meta-analysis of 225 studies published in PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/abs/10.1073/pnas.1319030111) by Freeman et al. (2014) compared active learning with traditional lectures in undergraduate STEM courses. Exam scores were about 6% higher in the active sections. Students in lecture-only classes were **1.5 times more likely to fail**: 33.8% failed under lecturing against 21.8% under active learning.

Here's my error. When I published this post in March, that sentence said active learners were "1.5x less likely to fail." That's the paper's ratio pointed the wrong way. It survived seven months, including a voice pass in June that edited this exact paragraph and left the number alone. Nobody caught it until the audit for this refresh went back to the paper.

A [2024 randomized crossover trial](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40670-024-02219-1) with 146 second-year medical students points the same way. In the interactive sessions, students scored 0.27 standard deviations higher on a test of learning than after the passive lectures.

The idea that passive learning *feels* better while teaching less comes from a different study. [Deslauriers et al. (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1821936116) compared two ways of teaching the same introductory physics content: active classes, and lectures by experienced, highly rated instructors. Students in the active classes learned more, and rated their own learning *lower*. The authors' reading is that students took the extra effort as a sign they were learning less. The 2024 medical trial didn't reproduce that gap, since its students learned more and felt they learned more. The two studies disagree on how active learning feels. They agree on the test scores.

None of these studies tested side projects. They compared sitting through a lecture with doing something during class. Going from there to "build a project instead of finishing the course" is my extrapolation. I think it holds, but it's mine.

Richard Feynman gets the credit for the next part. The so-called [Feynman Technique](https://fs.blog/feynman-technique/)<Sidenote>He never wrote it down as a method. The name and the step-by-step version come from study blogs decades after his death, around 2011.</Sidenote> says to explain the thing in plain words, and the places where you stall are the places you don't understand yet. Teaching forces you to close the gap between "I get it" and "I can explain it."

The wiki leans on the same idea. Every module ends with a recall check (see the bottom of [the agent loop module](/learn/agentic-systems/agent-loop/)): before you move on, you try to state each key idea from memory, then reveal it to compare. Rereading is easier, and that's why it sticks less.

## Why side projects accelerate your career

Side projects do more than teach you. They create a **visible portfolio of judgment**: proof that you can make decisions on your own.

<MermaidDiagram title="The maker flywheel" chart="graph TD\n    A[Build something] --> B[Hit real problems]\n    B --> C[Solve them]\n    C --> D[Gain deep knowledge]\n    D --> E[Share what you learned]\n    E --> F[Attract opportunities]\n    F --> A" />

When you ship a project, you show the things a list of technologies can't. You picked a database, a framework, a way to deploy, and you owned those calls. You weighed real trade-offs and committed to one, which is most of what senior engineering actually is. And you finished it, which is harder than starting it.

## The compound effect of building in public

Building and sharing in public compounds. You learn the thing by building it, and you pin it down by writing about it. Readers' questions then expose the gaps you can't answer yet, and the next project is where you fill them.

The wiki went through the writing half of that loop. It became the [post on shipping a high-accuracy agent](/blog/high-accuracy-agent-priorities/), which is sixteen modules squeezed into one priority list, with every section linking back to the module it came from. Sometimes the writing step sends you back to building: the [interactive components on this blog](/blog/interactive-blog-components/) exist because some claims in other posts were more than a paragraph could carry.

Each project makes the next one better, technically and in how you *think about problems*.

<Callout type="note" title="It doesn't have to be original">
You don't need to invent a new framework. Build a todo app, but make it *yours*. Choose the tech stack, design the UI, make the hard decisions. A well-executed familiar idea teaches more than an abandoned ambitious one.
</Callout>

## How to start

Perfectionism stops more projects than missing skill ever will. Pick a problem you actually have, so you're your own first user. Keep the first version to a weekend, ship the embarrassing MVP, and write one thing about it, even if it's just a README with the actual decisions. Small is fine. The calendar PWA behind [my post on tests](/blog/tests-gave-me-control/) was a personal sandbox for learning Next.js patterns, and it still gave me a whole post to write.

## The knowledge you can't Google

There's a type of engineering knowledge that doesn't exist in documentation. It lives in the gap between "I know how X works" and "I know what happens when X meets Y in production." Michael Polanyi called this [tacit knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tacit_knowledge), the kind of knowing you pick up through experience and can't fully put into words.

You can't read your way to this knowledge. You can only build your way there.

> "What I cannot create, I do not understand." — Richard Feynman (written on his blackboard at the time of his death, 1988)

So pick the thing you've been meaning to study, and build a bad version of it this weekend instead. You'll learn more from its bugs than from the course you keep not finishing.
