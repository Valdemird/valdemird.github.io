Speed that snaps from zero to full is exact and stiff. A ramp of a few hundredths of a second gives the character weight, as long as stopping is faster than starting and turning is faster still. Tune all three as times, because a time is what the player's hands can feel.

<h2 id="what-is-acceleration-friction">What are acceleration and friction in a platformer?</h2>

Acceleration is how quickly the character reaches its top running speed. Friction, or deceleration, is how quickly it stops once you let go. In code they are the same operation: every physics tick, move the horizontal speed a fixed amount toward a target. The target is the input direction times the top speed. The fixed amount is the rate, and the rate is the whole design.

My lab uses three rates and picks one by what the player is doing:

| The player... | Rate | Time in my lab |
|---|---|---|
| holds a direction | accelerate | 0.11 s from 0 to 340 px/s |
| lets go | brake | 0.07 s from 340 px/s to 0 |
| pushes the other way | turn | 0.06 s to lose the speed, then the normal 0.11 s the other way |

The sheet's own explanation is blunt about the trade-off: a few frames of ramp give a sense of weight, but they have to be very few. Braking faster than accelerating, and turning faster still, is what keeps the precision.

Air control is the same idea with two multipliers. In the air the lab keeps 80% of the accelerate and turn rates (`air_control = 0.8`), so you can still steer a jump. It keeps only 40% of the braking (`air_friction = 0.4`), so letting go mid-jump doesn't kill the momentum you jumped with.

<h2 id="acceleration-godot">How to code acceleration and friction in Godot 4</h2>

It starts in the parameter file, which has no accelerations in it, only times. The comments are in Spanish because the whole project is.

<CodeBlock filename="addons/platformer_kit/player/movement_params.gd">
```gdscript
@export_group("Carrera")
## Velocidad máxima horizontal.
@export_range(100.0, 800.0, 5.0, "suffix:px/s") var max_speed := 340.0
## Tiempo de 0 a velocidad máxima. Corto = responde al instante.
@export_range(0.01, 0.5, 0.005, "suffix:s") var accel_time := 0.11
## Tiempo de velocidad máxima a 0 al soltar. Más corto que acelerar = parada precisa.
@export_range(0.01, 0.5, 0.005, "suffix:s") var decel_time := 0.07
## Tiempo en perder la velocidad al invertir la dirección (giro rápido).
@export_range(0.01, 0.5, 0.005, "suffix:s") var turn_time := 0.06
# ...
## Fracción de aceleración disponible en el aire.
@export_range(0.0, 1.0, 0.01) var air_control := 0.8
## Fracción de frenado en el aire sin entrada (conserva la inercia del salto).
@export_range(0.0, 1.0, 0.01) var air_friction := 0.4
```
</CodeBlock>

The controller turns those times into rates on the spot: rate = top speed / time. With 340 px/s that's 3,091 px/s² to accelerate, 4,857 to brake and 5,667 to turn. Nobody tunes 4,857. You tune 0.07 s and let the division happen. The header of the same file makes the point about gravity: nobody thinks "gravity 1994 px/s²", you think "jumps 3.6 tiles and takes 0.38 s to reach the top".

<CodeBlock filename="addons/platformer_kit/player/player.gd">
```gdscript
var p := params
var target := input_x * p.max_speed * _walk_scale(on_floor)

# Versión ingenua: velocidad = dirección × máxima. Preciso pero robótico.
if not Lab.on(&"accel_curves"):
	velocity.x = target
	return

# ACELERACIÓN Y FRICCIÓN: tres tasas distintas según la intención del jugador.
var rate: float
if input_x == 0:
	rate = p.max_speed / p.decel_time                 # soltar: frenar rápido
	if not on_floor:
		rate *= p.air_friction                         # ...pero conservar inercia en el aire
		# ...
elif velocity.x != 0.0 and signf(velocity.x) != float(input_x):
	rate = p.max_speed / p.turn_time                  # invertir: giro aún más rápido
	if not on_floor:
		rate *= p.air_control
else:
	rate = p.max_speed / p.accel_time                 # acelerar
	if not on_floor:
		rate *= p.air_control
	# ...
if _at_apex(on_floor):
	rate *= p.apex_accel_mult                         # más control en el ápice
velocity.x = move_toward(velocity.x, target, rate * delta)
```
</CodeBlock>

With T, the function stops at the first `return`. `velocity.x = target` is the entire naive version, one line (`_walk_scale` is 1.0 while running). Everything below it is the technique, and a few details are worth reading twice:

- The turn branch only fires while the speed still points the other way (`signf(velocity.x) != float(input_x)`). Once it crosses zero, the normal acceleration takes over, so a full reversal costs 0.06 + 0.11 = 0.17 s.
- The air multipliers sit inside each branch, which is how braking gets 0.4 while steering gets 0.8.
- Near the top of a jump the rate gets one more boost, `apex_accel_mult` (×1.5). That's the apex hang from [the jump gravity post](/blog/jump-gravity-apex-hang/).
- The two `# ...` gaps are special cases. After a [wall jump](/blog/wall-jump/), letting go barely brakes at all, so the jump carries you to the opposite wall. And speed above the maximum, from a [dash](/blog/platformer-dash/) or a moving platform, bleeds off slowly instead of being cut.

<h2 id="celeste">Three rates or one: my lab against Celeste</h2>

Celeste does it with a single rate.<Sidenote>From [Player.cs](https://github.com/NoelFB/Celeste/blob/master/Source/Player/Player.cs), the player code the Celeste team put on GitHub. The constants are near the top of the file. C#, lightly trimmed.</Sidenote> `RunAccel = 1000f` is used for starting, stopping and turning, and in the air everything is multiplied by `AirMult = .65f`. A second rate, `RunReduce = 400f`, only applies when you're already above the top speed of `MaxRun = 90f` and still pushing the same way.

<CodeBlock filename="Celeste/Source/Player/Player.cs">
```csharp
float mult = onGround ? 1 : AirMult;
if (onGround && level.CoreMode == Session.CoreModes.Cold)
    mult *= .3f;

float max = Holding == null ? MaxRun : HoldingMaxRun;
// ...
if (Math.Abs(Speed.X) > max && Math.Sign(Speed.X) == moveX)
    Speed.X = Calc.Approach(Speed.X, max * moveX, RunReduce * mult * Engine.DeltaTime);  //Reduce back from beyond the max speed
else
    Speed.X = Calc.Approach(Speed.X, max * moveX, RunAccel * mult * Engine.DeltaTime);   //Approach the max speed
```
</CodeBlock>

`Calc.Approach` is Celeste's `move_toward`: the pattern is identical. To compare the tuning, convert to seconds, the only unit that survives the trip between two games. Celeste's character runs at 90 px/s and mine at 340, so 1,000 against 3,091 px/s² tells you nothing.

| | My lab | Celeste |
|---|---|---|
| Start, 0 to full speed | 0.11 s | 0.09 s |
| Stop after letting go | 0.07 s | 0.09 s |
| Full reversal, full speed to full speed | 0.17 s | 0.18 s |
| Start in the air | 0.14 s | 0.14 s |
| Stop in the air | 0.175 s | 0.14 s |

Celeste's times are `MaxRun / RunAccel`, doubled for the reversal and divided by `AirMult` in the air. The numbers come out close. The shape doesn't: Celeste brakes exactly as hard as it accelerates, everywhere. My lab brakes harder than it accelerates on the ground and much softer in the air. In both, a full reversal takes under a fifth of a second.

<h2 id="when-to-use">When acceleration and friction help, and when they hurt</h2>

| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| Narrow platforms, precise landings | Short ramps, braking faster than accelerating. At 0.07 s the lab's character skids about 10 px after you let go, a quarter of a tile. |
| Turning around mid-run | Make it the fastest of the three rates: 0.06 s in my lab. |
| Jumps | Brake less in the air than on the ground, so letting go doesn't kill the jump's momentum. |
| Ice and slippery floors, on purpose | A long ramp is the point. Celeste multiplies ground acceleration by 0.3 in its cold core mode (`CoreModes.Cold`). |
| A slow stop in a precision game | No. With 0.3 s to stop, a character at 340 px/s slides 51 px after you let go, more than a whole tile. |

<Collapsible title="Why move_toward and not lerp">
A common alternative is to write `velocity.x = lerp(velocity.x, target, k)` every tick. It closes a fixed fraction of the gap instead of a fixed amount, so it starts fast, slows down near the target and never quite arrives. There's no "time to full speed" to tune, only a `k` whose meaning changes if the tick rate does. That's a generic sketch, not code from my project.

`move_toward` closes a fixed amount per tick, gets to the target exactly, and the time it takes is the number you wrote in the parameter file. At the lab's 120 Hz, 0.11 s is 13.2 ticks, so in practice the character reaches 340 px/s on the 14th tick.
</Collapsible>

<h2 id="quick-answers">Quick answers</h2>

<h3 id="how-fast-accelerate">How fast should a platformer character accelerate?</h3>

Fast. My lab takes 0.11 s to reach full speed and Celeste 0.09 s. At 60 frames per second that's under seven frames in both.

<h3 id="stop-faster">Should stopping be faster than accelerating?</h3>

On the ground, if your platforms are narrow, yes: my lab brakes in 0.07 s against 0.11 s to start. Celeste uses the same rate for both and plays fine, so treat it as a decision you make on purpose.

<h3 id="air-control">What is air control in a platformer?</h3>

The fraction of the ground acceleration you keep in the air: 0.8 in my lab, 0.65 in Celeste. My lab also gives air braking its own number (0.4), so steering a jump and coasting through one are tuned separately.

<h3 id="time-to-acceleration">How do I turn a time into an acceleration?</h3>

Divide the top speed by the time. 340 px/s in 0.11 s is 3,091 px/s². Then each tick: `velocity.x = move_toward(velocity.x, target, rate * delta)`.

Open sheet 01, press T and stop on the two-tile platforms. You'll stop exactly where you let go, and the sheet's note admits it: instant speed is exact. Then turn around in the middle of the corridor and look at the inspector: a square wave with nothing in between. That's the cursor from the subtitle, and six hundredths of a second is all it takes to turn it back into a body.
